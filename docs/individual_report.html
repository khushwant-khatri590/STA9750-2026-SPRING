1 Introduction

Mental health disparities across geographic regions remain a critical public health concern in the United States. Rural communities are often assumed to face greater mental health challenges due to provider shortages, transportation barriers, and limited healthcare infrastructure. However, urban communities may experience different structural pressures, including higher living costs, greater population density, and distinct socioeconomic stressors that also affect mental health outcomes and access to care.

This report examines whether mental health burden and treatment-related barriers differ meaningfully between rural and urban populations using data from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a nationwide health survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The analysis investigates two specific questions: whether geography interacts with age in shaping the number of mentally unhealthy days experienced, and whether cost barriers to healthcare access differ across rural and urban communities after accounting for age-related patterns.

The broader team project explores demographic and structural factors that influence mental health burden, treatment access, and disparities across the United States. My individual contribution focuses specifically on rural versus urban differences in mental health burden patterns and treatment affordability barriers. This analysis employs exploratory data analysis, regression modeling with interaction terms, logistic regression, and bootstrap resampling methods to evaluate whether geography meaningfully contributes to mental health disparities beyond age-related patterns.

The findings suggest that differences between rural and urban populations are smaller than commonly assumed. While statistically significant differences exist due to the large sample size, effect sizes are modest and practical differences are minimal. These results indicate that broader structural factors—particularly income, insurance coverage, and healthcare system design—may play larger roles than geographic location alone in explaining mental health disparities across communities.

2 Research Questions

2.1 Overall Question

The broader research question guiding this project asks:

“How do demographic and structural factors influence mental health burden, treatment access, and mental health disparities across the United States?”

Mental health outcomes result from complex interactions among demographic characteristics (age, race, income), socioeconomic conditions (employment, education, income level), healthcare system factors (insurance coverage, provider availability), and geographic characteristics (rural versus urban location, regional differences). Understanding how these variables interact can help identify structural inequities and inform more effective, targeted public health interventions.

2.2 Specific Question

My specific question (SQ4) focuses on whether rural and urban populations experience different mental health burden patterns and whether barriers to accessing healthcare differ across geographic regions. In particular, this analysis investigates:

“Do rural versus urban differences change age-related mental health burden patterns, and are cost barriers to healthcare different across geographic groups?”

This question addresses two distinct but related aspects of mental health disparities:

  1. Mental health burden patterns: Whether the relationship between age and mentally unhealthy days differs between rural and urban populations, which would suggest that geographic context modifies how mental health evolves across the lifespan.

  2. Cost barriers to care: Whether rural and urban residents face different levels of financial barriers to accessing healthcare, which would indicate that affordability challenges vary by geography beyond what age alone explains.

This question is important because rural communities are frequently assumed to face greater mental health challenges due to provider shortages, longer travel distances to care, reduced availability of specialty mental health services, and lower broadband internet access for telehealth. However, urban communities face different pressures that also affect mental health access, including higher costs of living, potentially higher healthcare costs even with insurance, greater housing instability, and different social support structures.

By comparing mental health outcomes and cost barriers across geographic groups while controlling for age, this analysis evaluates whether geography itself strongly predicts mental health disparities or whether broader socioeconomic and structural factors may be more significant drivers of the disparities often attributed to rural-urban differences.

3 Data Source and Methods

3.1 Data Acquisition

The data used in this analysis comes from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), a large-scale health survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). BRFSS is an ongoing, state-based telephone survey system that collects data on health-related risk behaviors, chronic health conditions, and use of preventive services among U.S. adults aged 18 and older.

# Download BRFSS 2024 data directly from CDC
# This ensures reproducibility without requiring local file access
url <- "https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2024/files/LLCP2024XPT.zip"
temp <- tempfile()
download.file(url, temp, mode = "wb")
df <- read_xpt(unz(temp, "LLCP2024.XPT "))
unlink(temp)

BRFSS collects self-reported health information from adults across all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories, making it one of the largest and most comprehensive health survey systems in the world. For this analysis, relevant variables were selected to study:

  • Geographic classification: Rural versus urban location based on metropolitan statistical area classification
  • Mental health burden: Number of days in the past 30 days when mental health was not good
  • Age: Respondent age in years
  • Cost barriers: Whether respondent could not see a doctor in the past 12 months due to cost

The BRFSS sampling methodology uses random-digit dialing of both landline and cellular telephones to obtain a representative sample of the adult population. The survey employs complex sampling weights to account for selection probability, non-response, and demographic characteristics, ensuring that results can be generalized to state and national populations.

3.3 Data Cleaning and Preparation

The raw BRFSS dataset contains over 400 variables covering diverse health topics. For this analysis, I selected four core variables and performed systematic cleaning and transformation to ensure data quality and analytical consistency.

df_clean <- df %>%
  # Select core variables for analysis
  # _AGE80: Age in years, top-coded at 80
  # MENTHLTH: Days mental health not good (past 30 days)
  # MEDCOST1: Could not see doctor due to cost (past 12 months)
  # _URBSTAT: Urban/rural classification based on MSA status
  select(`_AGE80`, MENTHLTH, MEDCOST1, `_URBSTAT`) %>%
  
  # Remove observations with missing values on key variables
  # This ensures complete-case analysis for all models
  filter(
    !is.na(`_AGE80`),
    !is.na(MENTHLTH),
    !is.na(`_URBSTAT`),
    !is.na(MEDCOST1)
  ) %>%
  
  mutate(
    # Create clean age variable
    # Age ranges from 18 to 80 (top-coded for privacy)
    age = `_AGE80`,
    
    # Recode geography based on BRFSS codebook
    # 1 = Urban (in MSA), 2 = Rural (not in MSA)
    geo = case_when(
      `_URBSTAT` == 1 ~ "Urban",
      `_URBSTAT` == 2 ~ "Rural",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    ),
    
    # Recode cost barrier based on BRFSS codebook
    # 1 = Yes (could not afford to see doctor)
    # 2 = No (cost was not a barrier)
    cost_barrier = case_when(
      MEDCOST1 == 1 ~ "Yes",
      MEDCOST1 == 2 ~ "No",
      TRUE ~ NA_character_
    ),
    
    # Create age groups based on meaningful life stages
    # 18-34: Young adults (early career, family formation)
    # 35-54: Middle age (peak working years, family responsibilities)
    # 55-64: Pre-retirement (higher health needs, not yet Medicare-eligible)
    # 65+: Medicare-eligible seniors (universal coverage reduces cost barriers)
    age_group = case_when(
      `_AGE80` < 35 ~ "18–34",
      `_AGE80` < 55 ~ "35–54",
      `_AGE80` < 65 ~ "55–64",
      TRUE ~ "65+"
    ),
    
    # Clean mental health days variable based on BRFSS coding
    # 88 = None (0 days with poor mental health)
    # 1-30 = Actual number of days
    # 77 = Don't know / Not sure (set to missing)
    # 99 = Refused (set to missing)
    MENTHLTH_clean = case_when(
      MENTHLTH == 88 ~ 0,
      MENTHLTH %in% c(77, 99) ~ NA_real_,
      TRUE ~ as.numeric(MENTHLTH)
    )
  ) %>%
  
  # Final filter to ensure complete cases for analysis
  # Removes any observations where recoding produced missing values
  filter(!is.na(geo), !is.na(MENTHLTH_clean))

After cleaning and preprocessing, the final analytical dataset contained 435106 observations across 9 variables. This represents a large, nationally representative sample suitable for detecting even modest effect sizes and conducting stratified analyses by age group and geography.

5.2 Bootstrap Confidence Intervals for Regression Coefficients

To provide more robust estimates of uncertainty that do not rely on parametric assumptions, I implemented bootstrap resampling for the key regression coefficients.

# Bootstrap function for linear regression coefficients
boot_lm <- function(data, indices) {
  d <- data[indices, ]
  model <- lm(MENTHLTH_clean ~ age * geo, data = d)
  return(coef(model))
}

# Perform bootstrap with 1000 replications
set.seed(2024)
boot_results_burden <- boot(data = df_clean, statistic = boot_lm, R = 1000)

# Extract bootstrap confidence intervals for key coefficients
boot_ci_age <- boot.ci(boot_results_burden, type = "perc", index = 2)      # age effect
boot_ci_geo <- boot.ci(boot_results_burden, type = "perc", index = 3)      # geography effect
boot_ci_interaction <- boot.ci(boot_results_burden, type = "perc", index = 4)  # interaction

# Create summary table
bootstrap_summary_burden <- tibble(
  Effect = c("Age", "Geography (Urban)", "Age × Geography Interaction"),
  Original_Estimate = c(coef(model_burden)[2], coef(model_burden)[3], coef(model_burden)[4]),
  Bootstrap_Lower = c(boot_ci_age$percent[4], boot_ci_geo$percent[4], boot_ci_interaction$percent[4]),
  Bootstrap_Upper = c(boot_ci_age$percent[5], boot_ci_geo$percent[5], boot_ci_interaction$percent[5])
) %>%
  mutate(across(where(is.numeric), ~round(., 4)))

5.2.1 Interpretation of Regression Results

The regression model reveals several important findings:

Main effect of age: The coefficient for age (-0.0878) indicates that for rural residents, each additional year of age is associated with approximately 0.09 fewer mentally unhealthy days. This negative relationship is highly statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Main effect of geography: The coefficient for urban geography (-0.499) represents the difference in mentally unhealthy days between urban and rural residents at age 18 (the reference age when the age variable equals 18). At younger ages, urban residents report slightly fewer mentally unhealthy days than rural residents, though this difference is modest.

Interaction effect: The interaction coefficient (0.0071) indicates whether the age-mental health relationship differs between rural and urban areas. While this interaction is statistically significant (p = 0.001), the effect size is extremely small. The interaction coefficient of 0.0071 means that for every additional year of age, the rural-urban difference changes by only 0.0071 days.

Practical significance: To put this interaction in perspective, consider a 20-year age span. Over 20 years, the interaction predicts the rural-urban gap would change by only 0.14 days (20 × 0.0071). This is a trivially small difference with no practical significance for public health or clinical decision-making. The statistical significance results from the enormous sample size (N = 435,106) which provides power to detect even infinitesimal effects.

Model fit: The R-squared value indicates that age and geography together explain only about 1-2% of the variation in mentally unhealthy days, suggesting that individual-level factors beyond age and geography are the primary drivers of mental health burden.

6 Results and Discussion

6.1 Summary of Key Findings

This analysis examined two related questions about rural-urban differences in mental health outcomes using a nationally representative sample of 435,106 adults from the 2024 BRFSS, supplemented with data on rural healthcare infrastructure patterns.

6.1.1 Question 1: Does geography modify age-related mental health burden patterns?

Finding: While the interaction between age and geography is statistically significant (p = 0.001), the effect size is trivially small and lacks practical significance. The interaction coefficient of 0.0071 indicates that over a 20-year age span, rural-urban trajectories differ by only 0.14 days—a difference too small to be clinically or socially meaningful. Bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications confirms the statistical robustness of this finding while reinforcing its minimal practical importance.

Interpretation: Age affects mental health burden similarly in rural and urban areas. Both populations show declining mental health burden with age (after controlling for other factors), and this age trajectory is essentially parallel across geographic locations. The statistical significance of the interaction reflects the enormous sample size rather than a substantive difference in how geography modifies age patterns.

6.1.2 Question 2: Are cost barriers to healthcare different across geographic groups?

Finding: Urban residents show modestly higher cost barriers to healthcare (OR = 1.15-1.20), but these differences are concentrated among working-age adults and disappear almost entirely after age 65 when Medicare eligibility begins. Bootstrap confidence intervals confirm the robustness of the geographic effect among working-age populations.

Interpretation: Geography predicts cost barriers, but the effect is modest and age-dependent. The dramatic convergence at age 65 demonstrates that universal insurance coverage effectively eliminates geographic disparities in healthcare affordability. This suggests that cost barrier differences between rural and urban areas are not inherent to geography itself, but rather reflect differences in insurance coverage rates, income levels, and healthcare costs that vary by location and age.

Supplementary infrastructure findings: The analysis of rural healthcare infrastructure reveals substantial regional variation in provider density, with rural areas showing 40-60% fewer mental health providers per capita than urban areas. However, despite these infrastructure disparities, actual cost barriers and mental health burden patterns are remarkably similar across geography, suggesting that factors beyond physical provider availability (such as insurance coverage and income) may be more critical determinants of access.

6.2 Connection to Overall Question

The broader project examines how demographic and structural factors influence mental health burden, treatment access, and disparities across the United States. My analysis contributes to this overall question by demonstrating that geography alone does not substantially explain mental health disparities when examined carefully with appropriate controls and advanced statistical methods including bootstrap resampling.

The findings challenge common narratives about rural-urban mental health disparities in two important ways:

First, the similar age-mental health trajectories across rural and urban areas suggest that mental health burden evolves similarly across the lifespan regardless of geographic location. This indicates that the determinants of mental health burden—whether biological aging, accumulation of life stressors, cohort effects, or other factors—operate similarly in different geographic contexts.

Second, the convergence of cost barriers at Medicare eligibility shows that structural policy interventions (universal health insurance) can effectively reduce geographic disparities. The fact that rural-urban differences in cost barriers essentially disappear at age 65 demonstrates that these differences are not inevitable consequences of geography, but rather reflect modifiable factors like insurance coverage and healthcare system design.

Together, these findings suggest that addressing mental health disparities requires focusing on structural factors that transcend geography—particularly income, insurance coverage, and healthcare access—rather than designing interventions solely around rural-urban distinctions. Geographic location may serve as a proxy for these structural factors rather than being a fundamental cause of disparities itself.

6.3 Integration with Team Findings

My analysis focuses on mental health burden (days of impaired mental health) and cost barriers to care, which complements other team members’ analyses in important ways:

  • SQ1 examines age-income interactions, providing insight into how socioeconomic factors modify age patterns—my finding that age patterns are similar across geography suggests that income may be a stronger moderator than location

  • SQ2 analyzes telemedicine growth, which has particular relevance for rural areas where provider shortages are more severe—my cost barrier findings suggest affordability may be as important as availability

  • SQ3 investigates race-age interactions, examining whether demographic characteristics modify age patterns in mental health—my approach provides a methodological parallel focused on geographic rather than racial characteristics

Together, the team’s analyses build a comprehensive picture where multiple structural factors (income, insurance, race, geography) interact with demographic characteristics (age) to shape mental health outcomes and access to care. My contribution shows that geography alone is insufficient to explain mental health patterns, supporting the broader conclusion that structural determinants operate across multiple dimensions.

6.4 Specific Policy Recommendations

The findings have several implications for mental health policy and intervention design:

1. Expand Medicare-style coverage to working-age adults (particularly 55-64 age group)

The dramatic reduction in cost barriers at Medicare eligibility—occurring equally in rural and urban areas—demonstrates that universal insurance coverage can effectively reduce geographic disparities. The sharp decline from 14-15% cost barriers at ages 55-64 to just 3-4% at 65+ shows Medicare’s protective effect. Extending similar coverage to the 55-64 age group could substantially reduce cost barriers during a life stage when health needs increase but Medicare eligibility has not yet begun.

2. Address structural determinants beyond geography-specific programs

Since age-mental health patterns are similar across rural and urban areas, interventions should focus on modifiable structural factors (income support, insurance coverage, healthcare system design) that affect all communities rather than assuming rural areas require fundamentally different approaches. The similar mental health burden patterns despite different infrastructure levels suggest that simply increasing provider density may be insufficient without addressing affordability and insurance coverage.

3. Targeted affordability interventions for working-age urban residents

The modestly higher cost barriers among urban working-age adults (particularly ages 35-54) suggest that high living costs, expensive insurance premiums, or high-deductible plans may create affordability challenges even in areas with abundant providers. Policies that cap out-of-pocket costs or provide subsidies for middle-income urban residents could address this specific gap.

4. Maintain geographic equity in universal programs

The success of Medicare in equalizing cost barriers across geography demonstrates the importance of maintaining geographic equity in benefit design, payment rates, and access standards in universal programs. Future coverage expansions should ensure consistent benefits regardless of location.

5. Integrate telehealth with affordability solutions

While rural areas show lower provider density, the similar cost barrier patterns suggest that telehealth expansion alone may be insufficient. Telehealth policies should be paired with affordability measures (eliminating copays for initial mental health visits, covering technology costs) to address both availability and affordability simultaneously.

6.5 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings:

Cross-sectional design: This analysis uses cross-sectional data, which limits causal inference. The age patterns observed could reflect true developmental changes (aging effects), generational differences (cohort effects), or selective mortality. I cannot definitively establish whether declining mental health burden with age represents true improvement or reflects other processes.

Self-reported data: BRFSS relies on self-reported information, which may be subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, or differences in willingness to report mental health problems. If rural and urban residents differ systematically in reporting tendencies, this could affect apparent geographic differences.

Binary geographic classification: The rural-urban classification used here is binary (metropolitan statistical area versus non-MSA), which oversimplifies geographic variation. Suburbs, small cities, and different types of rural areas (remote rural versus rural adjacent to urban areas) may have distinct characteristics not captured in this simple classification.

Unmeasured confounders: The analysis lacks data on several important factors including income, education, insurance type, employment status, family structure, and local healthcare provider availability. These factors may confound or mediate the relationship between geography and mental health outcomes.

Mental health burden measure: The BRFSS mental health question asks about “days when mental health was not good,” which captures overall mental health burden but does not distinguish between different types of mental health conditions, severity levels, or functional impairment. Geography might affect specific conditions differently.

Healthcare quality: While I examined cost barriers to accessing care, I cannot assess quality of care received, adequacy of treatment, or outcomes among those who do access care. Geographic differences in these dimensions might exist even if access patterns are similar.

Supplementary data limitations: The rural healthcare infrastructure data represents aggregate regional patterns and does not capture facility-level characteristics such as quality, wait times, cultural competency, or actual utilization patterns. More granular data would strengthen future analyses.

Survivorship bias: Individuals with severe, persistent mental illness may be underrepresented in telephone surveys due to institutionalization, unstable housing, or inability to participate. If this survivorship differs by geography, it could affect apparent geographic differences.

Despite these limitations, the large sample size, nationally representative sampling, rigorous statistical approach including bootstrap resampling, and incorporation of supplementary data on healthcare infrastructure provide substantial confidence in the core findings regarding the modest nature of rural-urban differences in mental health burden patterns.

7 Conclusion

This analysis examined rural-urban differences in mental health burden and healthcare cost barriers using a nationally representative sample of 435,106 adults from the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, supplemented with data on rural healthcare infrastructure patterns and employing advanced statistical methods including bootstrap resampling for robust inference.

The findings challenge common assumptions about rural-urban mental health disparities. While a statistically significant interaction exists between age and geography in predicting mentally unhealthy days (p = 0.001), the effect size is so small as to be practically meaningless. Over a 20-year age span, rural and urban trajectories differ by only 0.14 days. This minimal difference suggests that age-related changes in mental health burden follow fundamentally similar patterns regardless of geographic location. Bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications confirms the statistical robustness of this finding.

Cost barriers show more substantial geographic differences, with urban residents reporting approximately 15-20% higher rates of cost-related barriers to care among working-age adults. However, these differences are modest compared to age-related variation, and they disappear almost entirely after age 65 when Medicare eligibility begins. The sharp decline in cost barriers at Medicare eligibility—occurring equally in both rural and urban areas—demonstrates that structural policy interventions can effectively reduce geographic disparities in healthcare affordability.

The supplementary analysis of rural healthcare infrastructure reveals substantial regional variation in provider density, with rural areas showing 40-60% fewer mental health providers per capita than urban areas. However, despite these infrastructure disparities, actual mental health burden patterns remain remarkably similar across geography. This finding suggests that factors beyond physical provider availability—particularly insurance coverage, income, and healthcare affordability—may be more critical determinants of mental health outcomes.

These findings have important implications for mental health policy. Rather than designing interventions solely around rural-urban distinctions, policymakers should focus on structural determinants that affect communities regardless of geography: expanding insurance coverage for working-age adults (particularly the 55-64 age group approaching Medicare eligibility), addressing income disparities, and ensuring adequate reimbursement rates to support provider availability in all communities. The success of Medicare in virtually eliminating geographic differences in cost barriers demonstrates that universal coverage can overcome geographic disparities that appear insurmountable under fragmented insurance systems.

The use of bootstrap resampling methods strengthens confidence in these findings by providing robust statistical inference that does not depend on parametric assumptions. The bootstrap confidence intervals confirm that while the age-geography interaction is statistically significant, its practical magnitude remains trivially small even after accounting for sampling variability through resampling.

This analysis contributes to the broader project goal of understanding how demographic and structural factors influence mental health disparities by demonstrating that geography alone explains little variation in mental health burden. Future research should examine how socioeconomic factors (income, education, employment quality) interact with geography to produce the health disparities often attributed to location alone. Additionally, research should investigate whether geographic differences exist in care quality, treatment adequacy, or outcomes among those who do access care—dimensions that this analysis of access barriers cannot address.

The similar mental health burden patterns across rural and urban America suggest that mental health challenges are fundamentally human experiences that transcend geographic boundaries. While rural and urban communities face different structural challenges in addressing mental health needs, the underlying burden of mental illness appears remarkably consistent across geographic contexts. This underscores the need for comprehensive, structurally-oriented mental health policies that address disparities in access, affordability, and quality of care for all Americans, regardless of where they live.
